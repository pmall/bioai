Protein to Pill

The $60 billion question

24 August 2026

Nature Reviews Drug Discovery — the field's own journal — published a perspective last week calling AI's impact on drug discovery "disappointingly limited." With roughly $60 billion invested and zero FDA approvals, the authors argued the field has been measuring the wrong things. The question is no longer whether AI can design molecules. It's whether anyone can show that AI-designed molecules work better than the ones humans design without them.

The benchmark problem

The critique from Bender et al. lands on a specific, correctable flaw: the field's benchmarks measure model validation, not decision-making improvement. A protein structure prediction model that beats AlphaFold on RMSD tells you something about the model. It tells you nothing about whether a drug company making decisions with that model produces better drugs than one without it.

This distinction matters because the value proposition of AI drug discovery was never "better predictions." It was "better medicines, faster." If AI produces molecules that bind tighter, fold more accurately, and screen faster — but the resulting drugs fail in clinical trials at the same rate as traditionally discovered ones — the AI contribution is cosmetic. The benchmark that matters is clinical, not computational.

What the numbers actually show

The most rigorous third-party count of the field's progress came from IntuitionLabs and ASCO in August: 117 AI-enabled therapeutic assets across 63 companies in interventional human trials. 60 have completed Phase 1. Eight have completed Phase 2. The Phase 1 success rate — 80-90%, roughly double the historical average — is the number most commonly cited in investor decks and press releases. It is also the least important number in the dataset.

Phase 1 asks whether a molecule is safe enough to keep studying. Phase 2 asks whether it works. The 6.8% Phase 2 completion rate is the honest measure of where the field stands, and it is not a number that suggests AI has fundamentally changed the clinical attrition problem. Phase 2 failure rates for AI-designed drugs are roughly 40% — comparable to the historical average for all drugs. AI compresses the timeline from target to Phase 1. It does not yet improve the odds of success once a molecule is in patients.

The Eroom's Law test

There is one data point that cuts against the pessimistic framing. Rentosertib — Insilico Medicine's fully AI-designed drug for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis — posted positive Phase IIa efficacy data: +98.4 mL FVC improvement versus -20.3 mL placebo at 12 weeks. Phase 3 has been initiated with 320 patients across 47 centers. If rentosertib succeeds in Phase 3, it would be the first approved drug designed entirely by AI, and the Eroom's Law counter-thesis — that AI accelerates discovery without improving clinical outcomes — would weaken considerably.

But rentosertib is one drug. The field has over 200 programs in clinical development, and one positive Phase IIa does not validate a thesis. What it does is eliminate one possible failure mode: the possibility that AI-designed molecules cannot work in patients at all. That question is now answered. The remaining question — whether they work better — is still open.

The capital tells the real story

The numbers behind the clinical pipeline reveal a different kind of problem. H1 2026 funding totaled roughly $2.64 billion across 12 qualifying announcements. Isomorphic Labs ($2.1 billion) and Chai Discovery ($400 million) accounted for 95% of the capital. The median round was $10 million. Half the announcements were seed or pre-seed, representing less than 2% of total capital. No pure-player AI drug discovery company secured financing between July 22 and August 5.

The "AI drug discovery boom" is, in reality, an Isomorphic and Chai boom. The rest of the sector is capital-starved. This concentration means the field's credibility rests on two companies that have yet to produce approved drugs — Isomorphic Labs has disclosed zero clinical candidates despite raising $2.1 billion, and Chai Discovery's pharma partnerships are still in the model-licensing phase. If either company fails to deliver, the entire sector's narrative collapses, because there is no diversified base of success to fall back on.

What better benchmarks would look like

The Bender et al. critique proposes a concrete fix: benchmarks that measure whether AI improves drug discovery decision-making. This would mean comparing outcomes for AI-assisted programs against matched controls — same target class, same patient population, similar starting compounds — and asking whether the AI-guided path produced a better result. Not faster. Not cheaper. Better.

Insilico Medicine's DDD Benchmark as a Service is a step in this direction, though it remains to be seen whether the industry will adopt it. The problem is that no company has an incentive to run the comparison honestly. If your competitive advantage is "our AI discovers drugs," you don't want a benchmark that measures whether the AI actually helped.

The honest framing

AI drug discovery has accomplished something real: it has compressed the timeline from target identification to clinical trials, demonstrated that computational design can produce molecules safe enough to test in humans, and attracted enough capital to build the infrastructure for the next phase. These are not nothing. But they are not what was promised.

The promise was not "faster molecules that fail in Phase 2 at the same rate." The promise was "better drugs, approved sooner." With $60 billion invested and zero approvals, the field owes its critics — and itself — an honest accounting of what AI has actually changed about drug discovery. The Nature Reviews perspective is that accounting. The field's response to it will determine whether the next $60 billion produces a different result.
