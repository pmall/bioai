Protein to Pill

The autoimmune cell therapy pivot

8 September 2026

Three patient deaths. Two clinical holds. Two companies pivoting entirely out of ex vivo cell therapy. In a single week, the autoimmune CAR-T thesis — the idea that the same CD19-targeting approach that works in blood cancers could reset the immune system in lupus, MS, and autoimmune encephalitis — hit its safety ceiling. The industry's response was not to fix the manufacturing process. It was to abandon it.

The safety signal

Novartis suspended eight autoimmune and neurology trials of rap-cel after three patient deaths from immune effector cell-associated hemophagocytic syndrome (IEC-HS). BMS paused its zola-cel autoimmune trials the same day — transient inflammatory events, no deaths, but enough of a pattern to halt enrollment. Both therapies are CD19-directed autologous CAR-T products built on rapid manufacturing platforms.

IEC-HS is a rare but severe hyperinflammatory response where immune cells expand uncontrollably, consuming the body's own blood cells. In oncology CAR-T, the risk is accepted because the alternative is often death from cancer. In autoimmune disease — where patients are often stable, not terminally ill — the risk calculus changes completely. A treatment that kills even a small fraction of patients is intolerable when the disease itself is manageable, if imperfectly.

The same day, a different direction

On the same day Novartis and BMS announced their pauses, Roche paid $75M upfront for Simcere's preclinical B cell trispecific — a three-target antibody, not a cell therapy at all. The deal is worth up to $1.53B in milestones. The message: B cell depletion is still the goal, but the delivery mechanism is shifting from living cells to engineered antibodies.

Antibodies don't expand uncontrollably in the body. They don't trigger IEC-HS. They don't require extracting a patient's cells, shipping them to a manufacturing facility, and reinfusing them weeks later. The trade-off is that antibodies require repeated dosing, while CAR-T is theoretically one-and-done. But one-and-done stops being attractive if it occasionally kills the patient.

The pivot

ArsenalBio halted all ex vivo CAR-T work for solid tumors and pivoted entirely to in vivo CAR-T — therapies that engineer immune cells inside the patient's body rather than outside it. TScan cut 75% of its staff, paused its Phase 3 and autoimmune program, and pivoted to in vivo TCR-T for solid tumors. Both companies concluded that the manufacturing complexity, T cell exhaustion, and poor tumor infiltration of ex vivo approaches made them commercially and technically unviable.

In vivo cell therapy eliminates the manufacturing bottleneck entirely. Instead of extracting cells, modifying them in a lab, and reinfusing them, the therapy delivers genetic instructions directly to immune cells in the body. J&J, Eli Lilly, and others are already building programs in this space. If it works, the cell therapy manufacturing infrastructure built over the last decade — the specialized facilities, the cold chain logistics, the GMP protocols — becomes stranded capital.

What the pivot reveals

The autoimmune CAR-T story was always a transplant from oncology. The logic was straightforward: if CAR-T works by depleting CD19+ B cells, and many autoimmune diseases are driven by autoreactive B cells, then the same approach should work. It did work — in small studies, in patients with severe refractory disease, and under conditions where the risk was tolerable.

But autoimmune disease is different from cancer in ways that matter. Autoimmune patients are not dying. They are often young. They have decades of life ahead of them, and a one-in-a-hundred chance of dying from the treatment is not the same proposition as when the alternative is stage IV lymphoma. The safety bar for autoimmune therapies is fundamentally higher, and the rapid manufacturing platforms that made CAR-T faster and cheaper may be producing cells that expand more aggressively than their slower-manufactured oncology predecessors.

The signals point to a future where B cell depletion for autoimmune disease is delivered by engineered antibodies, bispecifics, or in vivo cell engineering — not by ex vivo CAR-T. The field is not abandoning the goal of resetting the immune system. It is abandoning the delivery mechanism that made it too dangerous.
