The open-science protein engineering loop gets $95M

6 September 2026

The Allen Institute, University of Washington, and Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center launched AI BioDesign — a $95 million, five-year initiative to build an open-science design-build-measure-learn loop for protein engineering. The project will bring together 62 participants across institutions, focusing on closing the computation-biology cycle rather than producing clinical candidates.

What this is

AI BioDesign is not a drug discovery program. It's infrastructure: a shared, open-science effort to make the iterative loop between computational protein design and experimental validation work reliably. The goal is to turn protein engineering from a craft into a systematic process — where designs are generated computationally, built in the lab, measured, and fed back to improve the next round of designs.

At $95 million over five years, it's one of the largest open-science investments in protein engineering infrastructure. The participants come from multiple institutions, suggesting a collaborative rather than competitive model.

Why it matters

The protein design field has made extraordinary progress in computational methods — AlphaFold, ESMFold2, Boltz-2, and specialized tools like Aureka's OpenDDE have transformed what's possible in silico. But the Stanford AI Index 2026 quantified the gap: 85.87% expression success but only 9.65% binding success. Designs fold correctly but don't reliably bind targets.

This is the "last mile" problem in protein engineering — the gap between computational promise and experimental reality. Closing it requires not just better models, but better feedback loops: designs tested rigorously, results shared openly, and models improved based on real experimental outcomes.

The open-science angle

The initiative's open-science framing is significant. In a field where competitive advantage increasingly comes from proprietary experimental data — as Aureka demonstrated with its "release the model, keep the data" strategy — a shared, open approach to the design-build-measure-learn loop could accelerate progress for the entire community.

This follows other major open-science protein engineering investments: Northwestern's DREAM Cloud Lab (NSF-funded, 300,000+ proteins, open-access) and Adaptyv Bio's wet-lab capacity expansion (now $40M Series A). The pattern: as computational design becomes commoditized, the binding constraint shifts to experimental validation infrastructure — and that infrastructure is increasingly being built as shared, open-access resources.

What to watch

  • Whether the loop actually closes. The design-build-measure-learn cycle only works if feedback from experiments genuinely improves subsequent computational designs. Many initiatives promise this; few deliver it reliably.
  • Data sharing protocols. Open science requires open data. How experimental results are shared, structured, and made usable for model training will determine the initiative's impact.
  • Clinical relevance. The explicit exclusion of clinical candidates suggests this is pure infrastructure — valuable precisely because it's not chasing near-term therapeutic returns. But the field's credibility now hinges on clinical proof, not infrastructure.
