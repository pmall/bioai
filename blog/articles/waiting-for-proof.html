Protein to Pill

Waiting for proof

1 September 2026

AI drug discovery has spent August proving it can do almost everything — design proteins, generate molecules, run clinical trials, reach profitability. The one thing it has not yet done is produce an approved medicine. The field is now waiting for proof that matters.

The trial that hasn't started

Rentosertib — Insilico Medicine's antifibrotic, the field's most advanced AI-discovered drug — was estimated to begin its Phase 3 study on August 30. As of September 1, ClinicalTrials.gov still shows "NOT_YET_RECRUITING." The trial calls for 320 patients across 47 centers in China, with a 52-week treatment period. Phase 2a showed +98.4 mL FVC improvement versus placebo in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis.

The delay is not necessarily a red flag — clinical trial recruitment is routinely late, and China's regulatory environment has its own timelines. But rentosertib is not any drug. It is the first AI-discovered, AI-designed compound to reach Phase 3 efficacy testing. If it succeeds, it becomes the proof point the field has been building toward for a decade. If it continues to stall, the question of whether AI can deliver drugs that work in patients remains open — and the $7.3 billion in Insilico's total deal value starts to look like prepayment for assets that haven't yet been tested where it counts.

The first failure

BioXcel Therapeutics filed for Chapter 11 on August 28, signing a $57.5 million stalking-horse asset sale to Teva for IGALMI/BXCL501. The company had missed an Oaktree refinancing deadline on August 21, had $13.8 million in cash as of June 30, and was funded only through August. Shares fell 74.8%.

BioXcel is the first notable AI drug discovery company to fail. Its model — what it called "drug re-innovation," using machine learning to find new indications for existing drugs — is distinct from the de novo discovery model pursued by Insilico, Isomorphic, and Chai. BioXcel did not discover new molecules; it tried to repurpose known ones. The failure is specific to that model, not to AI drug discovery broadly. But it draws a sharp line: the companies that are gaining traction are those that use AI to design novel molecules, not those that use it to find new uses for old ones.

The honest assessment

A Nature Reviews Drug Discovery perspective published on August 24 delivered the field's most rigorous self-assessment to date. With approximately $60 billion invested and zero FDA approvals, the authors argued that AI's impact on clinically relevant outcomes is "disappointingly limited." The benchmarks that matter are not model performance scores but clinical decision-making improvement — whether AI changes what gets tested in patients and whether those patients do better.

The IntuitionLabs analysis from the same week put concrete numbers on the gap. Across 63 companies, 117 AI-enabled therapeutic assets are in interventional human trials. Sixty have completed Phase 1. Only eight — 6.8% — have completed Phase 2. The Phase 1 success rate of 80-90% is roughly double the historical average, confirming that AI compresses discovery timelines. The Phase 2 rate of approximately 40% is comparable to historical norms, confirming that AI does not yet improve clinical attrition. The bottleneck has shifted from "can we find molecules fast enough?" to "do they actually work in patients?"

What this means

These three threads — a trial that hasn't started, a company that failed, and an honest assessment from the field's own critics — describe the same moment. AI drug discovery has passed every test it set for itself. It can design proteins with twice the hit rate of traditional methods. It can generate hundreds of preclinical candidates. It can reach profitability through licensing. What it cannot yet do is point to a patient who is alive because an AI-designed drug treated their disease.

The field is not in trouble. The commercial models are working — Insilico's profitability, Chai's licensing to five major pharma companies, Isomorphic's $2.1 billion Series B. The science is advancing — blinded benchmarks in antibody design, sequence-only protein engineering at production scale, agentic workflows for drug discovery. But all of this is infrastructure. The proof that justifies the infrastructure is clinical, and it is still ahead.

Rentosertib will begin recruiting eventually. When it does, it will carry more weight than any AI drug discovery result before it. Not because it is the best-designed molecule, but because it is the first one good enough to be tested in 320 patients across 47 centers. The field is waiting for that trial to start — and for what it will eventually tell us about whether AI can do the one thing it was built to do: make medicines that work.
