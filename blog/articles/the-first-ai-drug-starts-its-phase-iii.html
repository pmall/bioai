Protein to Pill

The first AI drug starts its Phase III

10 September 2026

Nine days ago this blog described rentosertib's Phase III as the trial that hadn't started. It has now started. Insilico Medicine dosed the first patient in GENESIS-IPF-3, the 52-week study of its AI-discovered antifibrotic in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. It is the world's first Phase III trial of a generative-AI-driven drug — the moment the field moves from asking whether AI can generate a candidate to asking whether that candidate survives pivotal scrutiny.

What the trial is

GENESIS-IPF-3 (NCT07687459) plans to enroll 320 patients across 47 centers in China, with once-daily dosing over 52 weeks and a primary endpoint of annual decline in forced vital capacity. Peking Union Medical College Hospital and Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital dosed on day one, and principal investigator Zhe Xu has spoken of 3–4 years to approval if the result is favorable.

The announcement notes Insilico produced 9 preclinical candidates in the first 9 months of 2026 — the high-volume pipeline operating in parallel with its flagship program reaching late-stage testing.

Why this is the hard test

The Phase IIa result that got rentosertib here was 71 patients over 12 weeks, with 60 mg once daily showing +98.4 mL versus −20.3 mL on placebo — a secondary endpoint, measured over three months. The Phase III asks something stricter: slowing FVC decline over a full year, in more than four times as many patients.

The commercial bar has also moved since Phase IIa. Boehringer Ingelheim's nerandomilast arrived in October 2025 and generic nintedanib in April 2026, so rentosertib is no longer competing against two branded standards of care — it is competing against a newer mechanism and a cheaper generic at once. A 12-week lung-volume signal will not be enough; the drug has to show durable slowing of decline that justifies its place in that market.

What backs the run

Insilico enters the long trial with unusual resources for an AI drug company: $106.3M in first-half revenue, $35.5M in profit, $584.8M in cash plus $305M in converts raised September 3. That is the high-volume licensing model — Lilly, SK Biopharmaceuticals, Takeda, Servier — funding the pivotal bet rather than the other way around.

That funding matters because Phase III is where the field's central thesis gets tested. AI has already shown it compresses discovery: 80–90% Phase I success against a 40–65% historical average. Phase II rates look ordinary, around 40%. If rentosertib fails at 52 weeks, the argument that AI accelerates discovery without improving clinical outcomes gets stronger. If it succeeds, the counter-thesis to Eroom's Law — that R&D productivity can actually reverse — gets its first real data point.

What this means

First-patient-dosed is not efficacy. It is the start of a year of treatment plus follow-up and analysis, with approval years beyond that even on the optimistic timeline. Most Phase III programs that dose a first patient do not become drugs.

But the status change matters. Until this week, every claim about AI-discovered drugs carried the same caveat: none had been tested at pivotal scale. That caveat is now gone — replaced by a harder question with a fixed protocol, a registered endpoint, and 320 patients who will answer it. The field spent a decade proving AI can design molecules. The next three years test whether any of them work where it counts.
