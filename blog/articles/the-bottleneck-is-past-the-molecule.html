Protein to Pill

The bottleneck is past the molecule

9 September 2026

AI drug discovery spent five years learning to generate molecules. This week, three separate announcements pointed at what happens after the molecule exists — and suggested that is where the field now wins or loses. A company built around the "boring middle" of development, a $3 billion bet on getting prescriptions into patients, and a shift from fixing trials mid-course to designing them predictively. The binding constraint has moved downstream.

The boring middle

Formation Bio argues there is now an excess of clinical-stage molecules stuck in enrollment, site selection, and protocol amendments. The company, led by former Biogen and Pfizer executive Robert Ehlers as chief scientific officer, acquires or licenses deprioritized clinical-stage assets — including BLKR201, sprifermin, and KMR301 — and applies AI to development execution rather than molecule finding.

The framing inverts the usual AI pitch. The problem is not too few candidates. It is too many candidates moving too slowly through a development system that was never built for throughput. Formation describes the next decade's challenge as mapping disease-indication and patient-subset space onto existing molecular-intervention space — in other words, figuring out what the molecules we already have can actually treat, and proving it efficiently.

A third of prescriptions never reach the first dose

Forus raised $150M at a $3B valuation, led by Bain Capital Ventures with all existing investors reinvesting, to close the prescription-to-treatment gap. More than a third of patients on high-cost or complex medicines never receive their first dose — lost to insurance prior authorizations, financial assistance paperwork, and fulfillment logistics across drugs, payers, and pharmacies.

The platform is live in all 50 states and 85% of US ZIP codes, working with 9 of the top 15 global biopharmas. Valuation tripled in about four months. The thesis is blunt: faster launch and access lets biopharma invest in more drugs for more diseases — the commercialization layer, not discovery, is the binding constraint. AI agents that navigate coverage, assistance, and fulfillment are valued here at software multiples, alongside discovery platforms.

Trials designed before they start

Medidata describes the same downstream shift inside trial design itself: from reactive correction to predictive design. Phase I to approval now sits at 6.7%, while a single Phase III generates 5.9 million data points — up 283% in a decade. Mid-study amendments, long treated as inevitable, are now flagged as a core delay and cost driver that can be scenario-modelled before enrollment.

The tools are virtual twins — digital trial models that optimize protocol, eligibility, recruitment, dropout, and site performance pre-enrollment — and real-world-data external control arms, validated in oncology and cutting control-arm size by up to 33% in neurology studies. The caveat Medidata states plainly: AI is only as reliable as its training data coverage. But the direction is clear — trial risk moves from something managed mid-study to something priced before the first patient enrolls.

What ties the three together

Each story attacks a different downstream layer — development execution, trial design, post-approval access — but they share one assumption: molecules are no longer scarce. IQVIA's Predictive Clinical Development, claiming 33% faster startup, 1.7x more patients recruited, and 50% faster data cleaning, belongs to the same pattern. So do the operational tools that crossed accuracy thresholds this month for data extraction and safety review.

If open models commoditize discovery — and the open-source trajectory suggests they will — then development throughput and launch access decide how much science becomes medicine. The companies commanding the largest AI checks downstream are not promising better binders. They are promising fewer amendments, faster enrollment, and fewer prescriptions abandoned before the first dose. That is less glamorous than generative design. It is also where most drug programs actually fail.
