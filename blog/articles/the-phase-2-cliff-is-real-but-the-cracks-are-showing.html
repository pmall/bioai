The Phase 2 cliff is real — but the cracks are showing

19 August 2026

There is a number that should sit in the back of everyone's mind when reading claims about AI drug discovery: 6.8%. That's the percentage of AI-enabled therapeutic assets that have completed Phase 2 clinical trials, according to a July 2026 tracker. The field now has over 200 AI-designed drugs in clinical development, Phase 1 success rates running 80-90% — double the historical average — and exactly one fully AI-designed drug that has posted positive Phase IIa efficacy data. The cliff is real. But there are reasons to think it might be getting shorter.

The numbers, honestly

The landscape is large: 117 AI-enabled therapeutic assets across 63 companies have entered interventional human trials as of July 2026. Of those, 60 (51.3%) have completed Phase 1. But only 8 have completed Phase 2. The overall pipeline now numbers over 200 programs, with 25 companies actually delivering clinical candidates — not just publishing papers about potential candidates.

Those Phase 1 numbers are real and genuinely impressive. An 80-90% success rate against a historical average of 40-65% means AI-designed molecules are getting into patients and not causing unacceptable harm at roughly twice the rate of traditionally discovered compounds. But Phase 1 only answers one question: is this molecule safe enough to keep studying? The question that actually matters — does it work? — gets asked in Phase 2. And Phase 2 is where most drugs die, AI or not.

Rentosertib: the first crack in the cliff

The most important number in AI drug discovery right now is rentosertib's Phase IIa result. Insilico Medicine's fully AI-designed drug for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis posted positive efficacy data — the first time a drug that was conceived, designed, and optimized entirely by AI has shown it can work in patients. Phase 3 has been initiated with 320 patients across 47 centers in China.

This matters not because one drug succeeded, but because it answers a question the field has been dancing around for years: can AI produce molecules that actually treat disease, not just molecules that are safe enough to test? The answer is now yes, at least once. One result doesn't validate a thesis, but it does eliminate one possible failure mode. The AI-designed molecule crossed from "safe in patients" to "effective in patients" — and that transition is where the Phase 2 cliff lives.

Why the cliff might be shrinking

The Phase 2 cliff isn't an AI-specific problem. It's a biology problem: the gap between "this molecule hits its target" and "this molecule improves patient outcomes" is where most of drug discovery's value gets destroyed. But there are structural reasons to think AI might narrow that gap over time.

  • Better target selection. Recursion's validated neuroscience target — the first AI-discovered target advanced by a pharma partner — represents a different kind of bet than AI-optimized molecules against known targets. If AI can identify targets that are more likely to translate from bench to bedside, the Phase 2 failure rate drops before a molecule is ever designed.
  • Clinical trial design. Pathos AI's Foundry uses thousands of AI agents for trial design, patient matching, and dose optimization. The bottleneck is shifting from "find a molecule" to "prove it works in the right patients" — and AI is starting to work on that side too.
  • Smarter starting points. Insilico's DDD Benchmark as a Service addresses a real problem: most AI models are validated on benchmarks that measure prediction accuracy, not clinical relevance. By building a benchmark grounded in real drug discovery data, the field can start optimizing for the right thing.

The honest framing

Nature Reviews Drug Discovery published a perspective last month arguing that AI's impact on drug discovery is "disappointingly limited." The authors called for benchmarks that measure decision-making improvement, not just model performance. This is the right critique, and it lands harder now that the field has 200+ drugs in trials and one positive Phase IIa to point to. The question is no longer "can AI design molecules?" It's "can AI design molecules that work better than molecules designed without AI?"

The answer is: we're about to find out. Rentosertib's Phase 3 will read out around 2029. Recursion's REC-4881 Phase 2 data will be presented in November. Generate:Biomedicines' GB-0895 is in Phase 3 for asthma. These are the results that will determine whether the Phase 2 cliff was an artifact of immature pipelines or a fundamental limit of the approach.

What to watch

The market is pricing in optimism — $2.9 billion in 2026, projected to reach $13.8 billion by 2033 at 24.8% CAGR. Insilico is forecasting profitability in H1 2026 — the first AI drug discovery company to project making money. But profitability from licensing deals is different from profitability from approved drugs, and the field's credibility now hinges on the latter.

The Phase 2 cliff is real. The 6.8% completion rate is not a rounding error — it's the honest measure of where the field stands. But rentosertib's positive Phase IIa, Recursion's first validated target, and the emerging infrastructure for clinical trial optimization all suggest the cliff might be eroding at its edges. The next three years will tell us whether those cracks run deep.
