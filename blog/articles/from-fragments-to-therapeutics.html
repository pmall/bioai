Protein to Pill

From fragments to therapeutics

29 August 2026

For two years, AI antibody design has been demonstrated on minibinders — small antibody fragments useful as proof-of-concept but not as medicines. Last week, Chai Discovery crossed the threshold that matters: from fragments to full-length monoclonal antibodies, the format that actually becomes a drug. Five of the world's largest pharma companies have now licensed the same models. The question in antibody design is no longer whether AI can produce something that binds. It is which platform produces something that works in patients.

From minibinders to monoclonals

Chai Discovery raised $130M in a Series B round at approximately $1.3B valuation, bringing total funding above $225M. The round was led by General Catalyst and Oak HC/FT, with existing investors Thrive Capital, OpenAI, Dimension, and Menlo Ventures participating. But the number that matters is not the valuation. It is the technical claim underneath it.

Chai-2, the company's antibody design model, has moved from minibinders — antibody fragments typically used in research — to full-length monoclonal antibodies while maintaining hit rates. This is not an incremental improvement. Monoclonal antibodies are the format that FDA approves. They are the drugs that treat cancer, autoimmune disease, and infectious disease. A model that designs minibinders is a research tool. A model that designs full-length monoclonals is a drug discovery platform.

The advance has been applied to hard-to-drug targets including six GPCRs and a peptide-MHC complex — targets that have resisted conventional antibody design. Chai claims a 100-fold improvement over prior computational methods. CEO Josh Meier's framing is worth noting: "what looked like five-year problems just months ago are now getting solved in weeks."

Five pharmas, one shared model

What makes Chai's position structurally different from other AI antibody companies is the commercial model. Bristol Myers Squibb became the fourth large pharma to license Chai's antibody-design models, following Lilly, Novartis, and argenx. The same models are licensed non-exclusively to all four — and now a fifth partner. Discovery AI is priced as shared infrastructure, not proprietary advantage.

This is the opposite of how pharma has historically thought about competitive advantage. Usually, the molecule is proprietary and the tools are shared. With Chai, the tools are shared and the molecules will be proprietary — but the molecules belong to the pharma partners, not to Chai. Chai's moat is not exclusivity on the model. It is the data generated by five pharma partners running the same models across different targets, creating a feedback loop that improves the models for everyone.

BMS's collaboration is specifically described as building "an AI-powered, continuously learning discovery system" — not a one-off design project. This is infrastructure, not a vendor relationship.

The honest baseline

Chai's claims land against a backdrop that makes them both more credible and more scrutinized. The Stanford AI Index 2026 quantified the actual success rate across protein design methods: 85.87% of designs express correctly — the sequences fold as intended — but only 9.65% achieve binding. The best method, BoltzGen, reached 28.57%.

Chai's claim of 100-fold improvement over prior computational methods must be measured against this baseline. If prior methods were in the single-digit binding percentages, a 100-fold improvement would be remarkable. If they were already at 20-30%, the framing matters less than the absolute number. The AIntibody benchmark — the first blinded, independently validated competition — provides the first objective standard for measuring such claims. Aureka's winning antibody reached 94.7 pM affinity, a 2,000-fold improvement over its parent. The benchmark is now the yardstick.

What this changes

The significance of Chai's advance is not just technical. It restructures the competitive landscape in antibody design.

Before this, AI antibody design existed in two parallel tracks. On one track, specialized companies (Aureka, Chai, AbCellera) built proprietary models and validated them through internal wet labs. On the other track, open-source models (ESMFold2, Boltz-2, OpenDDE) matched or exceeded proprietary alternatives on public benchmarks. The moat was assumed to be proprietary data.

Chai's move to full-length monoclonals, combined with the five-partner shared-infrastructure model, introduces a third track: platform licensing as the commercial model. The company does not need to build its own pipeline. It needs five pharma partners to build theirs using Chai's models, generating data that makes the models better, attracting more partners. The flywheel is not molecule-centric. It is infrastructure-centric.

The remaining question is clinical validation. With rentosertib in Phase III, 117 AI-enabled drugs in trials, and zero FDA approvals, every claim about antibody design capability will be measured against what happens in patients. Chai has moved from fragments to therapeutics at the computational level. The next test is whether its full-length monoclonals reach clinical candidates — and whether those candidates reach endpoints.

Five pharma partners are betting that they will. The models they are licensing are the same ones. The data they generate will be different. The answer to whether AI-designed antibodies work as drugs will come from the clinical programs those partnerships produce — not from any single company's claims about hit rates.
