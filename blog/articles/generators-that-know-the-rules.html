Protein to Pill

Generators that know the rules — and when they don't

13 September 2026

Molecule generators are easy to build and hard to trust. Two papers this week attack the trust problem from opposite ends: one teaches the generator explicit chemical rules, the other demands calibrated uncertainty before letting a language model steer a self-driving lab. Together they describe what a trustworthy discovery loop actually requires.

Learning the rule, then generating inside it

Symbolic Neural Generation, published in Machine Learning, pairs a symbolic learner with a language-model generator. The symbolic side uses restricted inductive logic programming over posets, with a Bayesian heuristic and Latin Hypercube narrowing, to learn feasibility rules from known actives. The generator — a small Molecule-GPT2 model with 87 million parameters trained on 480 million ZINC compounds — is then constrained to SMILES strings that provably satisfy those rules.

On JAK2 and DRD2 the system performs comparably to state-of-the-art generators. The telling case is dopamine beta-hydroxylase (DBH), a data-sparse target with only five known inhibitors. There, SNG reaches clinical-candidate-level predicted affinity under GNINA scoring — and the 87M-parameter specialist beats GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet on the same task. Total API cost was around $10. Chemists judged several outputs synthesis-viable, and because the constraint is a logic filter, it can be inspected: you can read the rule the molecule was forced to obey. Code and data are open.

The point is architectural. Instead of asking a large model to absorb chemistry implicitly and hoping it generalizes, SNG learns an explicit, checkable rule first and generates inside it. On sparse targets — exactly where drug discovery lives — the small constrained model beats the large fluent one.

Literature priors only help with error bars

A perspective in Nature Machine Intelligence sets the calibration standard for the other half of the loop: using large language models as literature-prior surrogates inside Bayesian optimization and self-driving labs. The verdict is conditional. LLM priors help closed-loop discovery only when uncertainty is calibrated — naive fluency is not an error bar.

The prescription is concrete: ensembles, prompt perturbation, token-probability aggregation, and conformal prediction, treating the next-token distribution as a hypothesis distribution. The paper proposes auditing the full proposal-calibration-decision-execution loop rather than any single prediction. And it warns that offline benchmarks are weak predictors of closed-loop regret — a model that looks good on a static dataset can still steer an autonomous lab badly.

Read alongside SNG, the message sharpens. SNG constrains what gets proposed; calibration constrains how much the lab should believe each proposal. One without the other leaves the loop untrustworthy: valid molecules pursued in the wrong order, or well-ranked molecules that violate chemistry.

The contrast: a launch with no numbers

Apple's SimpleDesign, reported via 9to5Mac, claims a streamlined joint sequence-and-structure protein generator that collapses the usual two-step design process into a coupled problem to avoid sequence-structure mismatch. No parameter counts, training data, benchmarks, wet-lab validation, or code and weights were disclosed.

Its significance is who is entering, not what was shown — big-tech biology entry alongside the full-stack pattern. Against this week's two papers it reads as the thing they argue against: generation without inspectable rules and without calibrated uncertainty. A coupled generator may well fix real mismatch problems, but until there are numbers and molecules, it sits outside the trust standard the field is converging on.

What trustworthy looks like now

The bar emerging from these two papers is higher than "it generates plausible molecules." A generator earns its place in a discovery loop when its constraints are explicit enough to inspect, its uncertainty is calibrated enough to plan experiments against, and its value is measured in closed-loop regret rather than offline scores. SNG clears the first at $10 with open code; the calibration paper defines the second and third. That is a standard worth holding every new entrant to — including the ones with the largest logos.
