Protein to Pill

Thirty-four years to approval

4 September 2026

China's NMPA has approved Mprosevir, the first AI-assisted Class 1 innovative drug approved anywhere in the world. The molecule was discovered through DNA-encoded library screening of 49 billion compounds, narrowed by AI to 9 candidates in days, with 6 showing strong activity. Total time from candidate discovery to clinical trial completion: 3.5 years. The technology behind it — DEL screening — was first proposed in 1992. It took 34 years to reach a market.

What kind of AI got approved

Mprosevir is not a molecule designed by a generative model from scratch. It is a small molecule identified through DEL technology — a combinatorial chemistry approach that synthesizes billions of DNA-tagged compounds, exposes them to a biological target, and reads out which ones bind. The AI's role was to screen the enormous candidate space and prioritize molecules for synthesis and testing. Westlake University's team used AI to narrow from billions to dozens, then spent two years optimizing the lead molecule WLU6937 into a drug.

This is not the AI drug discovery story that dominates headlines — large language models generating novel proteins, diffusion models designing small molecules from scratch, or foundation models predicting binding affinity. It is a 34-year-old screening concept accelerated by machine learning. And it is the first one to reach patients.

The contrast with rentosertib

Rentosertib, Insilico Medicine's AI-discovered and AI-designed pan-KRAS inhibitor for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, is the field's most advanced de novo AI drug. It completed a positive Phase IIa with a 98.4 mL FVC improvement at 60 mg QD vs. -20.3 mL placebo. Its Phase III (NCT07687459) was estimated to begin on August 30. As of this week, ClinicalTrials.gov still shows NOT_YET_RECRUITING — 320 patients, 47 centers in China, 52-week treatment. The estimated start date has passed and enrollment has not begun.

The most advanced AI-designed drug in the world has not started its pivotal trial. Meanwhile, a DEL+AI molecule — built on a technology older than the internet — has already been approved.

What this tells us

The gap between these two milestones is not a failure of AI. It is a clarification of what AI actually does in drug discovery today.

DEL+AI works because it solves a concrete, well-defined problem: searching an enormous combinatorial space for molecules that bind a known target. The problem is large but tractable. The target is defined. The readout is binary — does it bind or not. AI excels at this kind of search.

De novo AI drug design — generating novel molecules with desired properties from scratch — solves a fundamentally harder problem. The molecule must bind, but it must also be synthesizable, have acceptable pharmacokinetics, avoid toxicity, and survive clinical testing. Each additional constraint multiplies the difficulty. The Nature Reviews Drug Discovery perspective that called AI's impact "disappointingly limited" pointed precisely at this gap: the distance between computational success and clinical proof.

China's regulatory system has drawn a clear line. The NMPA approved a molecule that was discovered through a known, validated technology class — DEL — with AI serving as an accelerator. It has not yet approved a molecule designed entirely by AI from a blank page. That distinction may matter more than the headlines suggest.

The broader picture

Mprosevir's approval arrives in a week of mixed signals for AI drug discovery. Superluminal Medicines raised a $60M Series B for an AI-designed MC4R agonist targeting GPCRs — a notoriously difficult target class representing ~35% of approved drug targets. US and European biopharma startups raised $12.6B in H1 2026, a five-year high, but the capital is flowing to later-stage companies with drugs in testing, not to early-stage platforms.

The pattern is consistent: AI drug discovery is succeeding at accelerating known processes (DEL screening, virtual screening, ADMET prediction) while the harder problem — generating genuinely novel therapeutics that work in patients — remains unresolved. The first approved AI drug is evidence of this, not a contradiction of it. DEL screening was already a proven technology. AI made it faster. That is a real and valuable contribution. It is just not the revolution that was promised.
