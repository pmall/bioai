Protein to Pill

The physical bottleneck

26 August 2026

AI drug discovery has spent three years solving a computational problem: how to design better molecules, faster. That problem is largely solved. The bottleneck that remains is physical — and this week, two developments show the industry starting to address it.

DNA synthesis, on premises

Telesis Bio's Gibson SOLA platform is being licensed by multiple AI-native drug discovery companies for on-premises DNA synthesis. The platform enables a 36-hour cycle from AI hypothesis to experimental result — enzymatic synthesis, no external DNA supply chain, and output of hundreds to thousands of constructs per day for antibody expression.

The detail that matters is "on-premises." The traditional DNA synthesis supply chain involves external vendors, shipping delays, and quality variability. For closed-loop AI labs — where the goal is to run hundreds of design-synthesize-test cycles per week — that external dependency becomes the binding constraint. Telesis's approach removes it. The AI designs the construct, the synthesizer produces it in-house, and the wet lab tests it, all within a single day and a half.

This is what closed-loop AI drug discovery actually looks like when it works: not a model that predicts binding affinity, but a physical pipeline that can turn a prediction into a tested molecule in 36 hours. The computation was never the hard part. The hard part was the plumbing.

From design to manufacturing

Cytiva's CTO published a forward-looking piece arguing that generative AI's impact is extending from drug discovery into bioprocessing and manufacturing. The signals are concrete: AI models predicting manufacturability — how easily a protein can be expressed, purified, and formulated — reducing downstream risks before a molecule ever reaches a bioreactor. Tahoe Therapeutics raised $30 million to build AI models of cells, targeting the kind of virtual cell simulation that DeepMind has said it wants to achieve within five years.

The piece proposes federated learning as the solution for the "low n" problem in bioprocessing — each manufacturing run generates vast data, but the number of runs is small. The vision is fully in-silico process development and digital twins for biomanufacturing. Whether that vision is five years away or ten, the direction is clear: AI drug discovery is expanding from "design molecules" to "design and produce molecules."

The infrastructure is catching up

These two developments — DNA synthesis speed and manufacturing AI — are pieces of the same puzzle. The closed-loop AI drug discovery pipeline has three physical steps: synthesize DNA, express and test the protein, manufacture at scale. Each of these has been a bottleneck that computation alone couldn't solve.

Telesis addresses the first step. Cytiva addresses the third. The middle step — high-throughput protein expression and testing — is being attacked by companies like Vivodyne, whose HIVE robotic labs can run 3.1 million tissue trials per year. Together, these capabilities form a physical infrastructure layer that complements the computational layer the field has spent years building.

The pattern is familiar from other technology transitions. Software ate the world, but only after the hardware — servers, networks, mobile devices — became fast and cheap enough to run it. AI drug discovery is following the same trajectory: the algorithms are ready, and now the physical infrastructure is catching up. The bottleneck has shifted from "can we design the right molecule?" to "can we synthesize, test, and manufacture it fast enough to matter?"

What changes

The implications are practical, not theoretical. A 36-hour design-synthesize-test cycle means an AI campaign can run hundreds of iterations in the time it used to take one. Manufacturing AI means the transition from discovery to production — historically a multi-year, multi-hundred-million-dollar process — starts compressing at the design stage. Digital twins mean process optimization happens in silico, not in stainless steel.

These are not breakthroughs that make headlines. They are infrastructure improvements that make the field work. The next wave of AI drug discovery will not be defined by better models. It will be defined by whether the physical layer can keep up with what the models can now do.
