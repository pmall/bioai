Protein to Pill

Who gets the credit

23 August 2026

Insilico Medicine's press releases say rentosertib was "discovered by" its AI platform. The patent names five humans as inventors. Both statements are true, and the gap between them is a legal vulnerability that could undermine the commercial foundation of AI drug discovery.

The contradiction

MIT Technology Review investigated the inventorship problem facing AI drug companies. Insilico Medicine, the most prominent AI-first drug discovery company, has consistently described rentosertib — its lead candidate, now in Phase III for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis — as discovered by its AI platform. In press releases, investor materials, and public statements, the AI is credited as the discoverer. On the patent filing, five human beings are listed as inventors.

This is not a mistake. It is a deliberate legal strategy, and it reveals a tension at the heart of AI drug discovery that has no resolution yet: if an AI system designs a drug molecule, who invented it?

What the law says

The legal answer, for now, is straightforward. In 2022, the US Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit ruled in DABUS that only natural persons can be named as inventors on US patents. The case involved Stephen Thaler, who argued that his artificial intelligence system DABUS had autonomously invented a food container and a light beacon. The court disagreed: under the Patent Act, an "inventor" is an individual, not a machine. The USPTO under the current administration has taken a pragmatic approach — AI is treated as a tool, and inventors need not disclose the use of AI in their work.

This creates a peculiar situation. An AI system can do the creative work that traditionally defines inventorship — generating novel molecular structures, predicting their properties, optimizing for desired characteristics — but the law requires that a human be credited as the inventor. The human must have "conceived" the invention. If the AI did the conception, and the human merely directed or implemented it, the question of who actually conceived becomes genuinely ambiguous.

The invalidity risk

Ryan Abbott, the attorney who argued the DABUS case and has written extensively on AI inventorship, warns that incorrect inventor listing could invalidate patents. If the named inventors did not actually conceive the invention — if the AI did, and the humans were listed as a legal formality — the patent may be vulnerable to challenge. This is not a theoretical risk. Competitors facing infringement claims routinely challenge patent validity, and inventorship is a known attack vector.

The stakes are significant. Rentosertib is Insilico's most advanced asset, targeting a market worth billions. If the patent is invalidated on inventorship grounds, the company loses exclusivity. For a company that has raised hundreds of millions of dollars on the strength of its AI platform and the patents it produces, this is an existential vulnerability — not because the science is wrong, but because the legal framework for attributing AI-generated inventions doesn't exist yet.

How companies are responding

The current approach across the industry is caution. Companies are keeping humans in the loop and documenting carefully. Insilico and others ensure that human scientists are involved at every stage of the drug design process — defining the problem, directing the AI, evaluating results, making decisions about which candidates to advance. The documentation emphasizes human judgment and decision-making, even when the AI generated the molecular structure.

Isomorphic Labs, which has yet to disclose a clinical candidate, has been notably careful about how it describes the role of AI in its work. The company's legal team is almost certainly aware that the inventorship question will eventually be tested in court, and they are positioning accordingly. Other companies — Recursion, Schrödinger, Chai Discovery — face the same issue, though none have yet advanced an AI-discovered drug far enough for the question to become urgent.

The broader implication

The inventorship problem is a specific instance of a larger pattern: the legal and regulatory frameworks governing drug development were designed for a world where humans do the inventing. AI drug discovery doesn't fit that framework, and the framework hasn't been updated. The USPTO's decision not to require disclosure of AI use is a stopgap, not a solution. It allows companies to avoid the question today but doesn't resolve it.

As AI systems become more autonomous in drug design — Claude designing binders across 15 targets with minimal human input, closed-loop workflows iterating through hundreds of variants with AI choosing the next experiment — the gap between what AI actually does and what the law requires humans to have done will widen. The question is not whether this tension will be tested in court. It is when, and what the court will decide.

For now, companies are navigating the ambiguity by documentation and human involvement. But this is a patch, not a fix. The commercial foundation of AI drug discovery — patents that protect the molecules AI designs — rests on a legal attribution that may not survive scrutiny. The field's credibility with investors, partners, and regulators depends on resolving this, and the resolution requires new law, not creative compliance.

The AI discovered the drug. The human is named as inventor. Both statements are true. The gap between them is where the field's next crisis will come from.
