Protein to Pill

The first profit

28 August 2026

Insilico Medicine reported $106.3 million in revenue and a $35.5 million net profit for the first half of 2026 — the first time any AI drug discovery company has reported full-period profitability. Revenue was up 287% year-over-year. Gross margin was 90.3%. The company finished the period with $584.8 million in cash.

These are not numbers the field expected to see this soon. AI drug discovery has been a story of burning capital — Isomorphic Labs raised $2.1 billion, Chai Discovery raised over $225 million, and the median funding round across the sector was roughly $10 million. Profitability was always "next year." Insilico made it this half.

What the numbers actually mean

The revenue is almost entirely deal-based: $103.1 million from licensing and collaboration agreements, $2.7 million from software. The total deal value across Insilico's partnerships now exceeds $7.3 billion — Lilly ($2.75 billion), SK Biopharmaceuticals ($2.5 billion), Takeda (~$600 million), Servier ($888 million). The profit is real but it is episodic: upfront payments and milestones arrive in lumps, not as recurring revenue.

This matters because the high-volume licensing model — generate many preclinical candidates, partner them at preclinical stage, collect upfront and milestone payments — is the commercial bet Insilico has made since its founding. The model does not require getting a drug through Phase 3 and to market. It requires generating enough candidates that enough partners pay enough upfronts to cover the cost of generating them. At $106.3 million in half a year, the math is working.

Insilico now has 33+ preclinical candidates and 13 IND clearances. The company is averaging one new development candidate per month. The pipeline is real. The question is whether the revenue is sustainable — whether the next set of deals will produce similar upfronts, or whether the largest deals (Lilly, SK, Takeda) were front-loaded and the remainder will be smaller.

What it means for the field

Three things follow from this result.

The high-volume licensing model is commercially viable. For two years, the debate in AI drug discovery has been between two models: high-volume licensing (Insilico) and platform-to-pipeline (Isomorphic, Recursion, Generate). Insilico's profitability is the first concrete evidence that one of these models can produce revenue. Isomorphic has no disclosed clinical candidates despite $2.1 billion in funding. Recursion is early in its pipeline. Insilico is now profitable. The commercial proof matters more than any benchmark.

Revenue from deals is different from revenue from drugs. Insilico's profit comes from licensing fees and milestone payments, not from selling a drug. The $103.1 million in deal revenue is real money, but it is tied to the continued willingness of partners to pay for preclinical assets. If rentosertib — Insilico's most advanced drug, now entering Phase 3 — succeeds, the deal revenue becomes secondary to product revenue. If it fails, the licensing model remains viable but the ceiling is lower: there is a limit to how many preclinical assets the market will pay for.

The field's first profitability test is not clinical — it's financial. Insilico has answered the question "can an AI drug discovery company make money?" with a yes. The next question is whether the money comes from drugs or from deals. Rentosertib's Phase 3 trial — 320 patients, 47 centers in China, 52-week treatment — will determine whether Insilico transitions from a licensing company to a drug company. That transition is where the real value lies, and it is still ahead.

The rentosertib question

Rentosertib remains the field's most important clinical program. It is the first AI-discovered, AI-designed drug to reach Phase 3 efficacy testing. Phase 2a showed +98.4 mL FVC improvement versus -20.3 mL placebo at 12 weeks in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. The Phase 3 estimated study start date of August 30 has passed, with registry status still showing "NOT_YET_RECRUITING."

If rentosertib succeeds, Insilico transitions from a licensing company with profitability to a drug company with a product. The deal revenue becomes the foundation, not the ceiling. If it fails, the licensing model still works — Insilico can continue generating candidates and collecting upfronts — but the narrative that AI can deliver drugs that work in patients weakens, and the $7.3 billion in deal value looks like prepayment for assets that didn't pan out.

Insilico's profitability is a milestone the field needed. It proves the commercial model can work. But the milestone is a foundation, not a destination. The real test — whether AI-designed drugs reach patients — is still ahead, and rentosertib is the trial that will decide it.
