Protein to Pill

Beyond the ADC

5 September 2026

Antibody-drug conjugates have dominated the conjugate therapeutics space for a decade. This week, HUTCHMED licensed HMPL-A830 to GSK for $1.3 billion — and it isn't an ADC. It's something new: an antibody-targeted therapy conjugate, or ATTC, pairing a KRAS inhibitor payload with an anti-EGFR antibody to address resistance in colorectal, pancreatic, and lung cancers. The deal signals that the conjugate modality is branching beyond cytotoxic payloads, and that Chinese biotech is now originating the platform innovations, not just executing on Western ones.

What an ATTC actually is

An ADC delivers a cytotoxic payload — kill the cell, broadly — guided by an antibody that finds a surface marker. An ATTC works differently. The antibody targets EGFR, a receptor overexpressed on many solid tumors, but the payload isn't a poison. It's a KRAS inhibitor. The idea is to solve a specific clinical problem: KRAS inhibitors work when tumors harbor certain KRAS mutations, but resistance emerges through pathway reactivation and feedback loops. By co-delivering the inhibitor directly to EGFR-expressing cells, the ATTC aims to maintain intracellular drug concentrations while the antibody blocks the EGFR-mediated resistance pathway from outside.

HMPL-A830 is preclinical and expected to enter trials in the second half of 2026. GSK gets ex-China rights; HUTCHMED retains Greater China. The $110 million upfront is modest relative to the $1.295 billion in milestones and tiered royalties — a structure that says GSK believes in the mechanism but is paying for proof.

A new conjugate category

The ADC market is already grappling with resistance. Genentech this month licensed DualityBio's DUPAC platform — novel-payload ADCs designed to work in tumors resistant to topoisomerase inhibitor-based ADCs like Enhertu and Trodelvy. The ADC field is splitting into generations: first-generation cytotoxic payloads, second-generation novel mechanisms, and now conjugates that carry targeted therapies rather than poisons.

The ATTC concept extends this logic further. Instead of replacing one cytotoxic payload with another, it pairs a targeted therapy — a molecule that inhibits a specific oncogenic driver — with an antibody that ensures delivery and blocks a resistance mechanism simultaneously. If the approach works, it creates a combination product where the antibody and small molecule aren't just co-administered but co-delivered, maintaining a fixed ratio and ensuring both reach the same cells.

Chinese biotech as platform originator

HUTCHMED is the company that brought fruquintinib to market globally — a selective VEGFR inhibitor for colorectal cancer. But the ATTC platform is a different kind of contribution. This isn't a me-too molecule or a regional license. It's a novel conjugate modality designed to address a resistance mechanism that limits one of the most important classes of targeted cancer therapies.

The pattern extends beyond HUTCHMED. DualityBio's novel-payload ADC platform attracted Genentech for a deal worth over $1 billion in milestones. Convergen raised $15 million for TRIM21 molecular glue degraders targeting protein aggregates in neurodegenerative diseases — a differentiated E3 ligase approach distinct from the VHL/CRBN-dominated PROTAC space. Chinese biotech companies are no longer just building faster versions of Western platforms. They are originating modalities and mechanisms that major pharma licenses for global development.

What it means for the conjugate landscape

The ADC market is projected to exceed $30 billion by 2030. But the interesting question isn't the size of the current market — it's how many subcategories will emerge within it. ATTCs represent one branch: targeted therapy payloads guided by resistance-blocking antibodies. Novel-payload ADCs like DualityBio's DUPAC represent another: next-generation cytotoxics designed to overcome first-generation resistance. And the broader induced proximity space — PROTACs, molecular glues — is converging with the conjugate concept as companies explore antibody-targeted degraders.

The HUTCHMED-GSK deal matters less for its dollar value than for what it validates: that conjugate therapeutics are a design space, not a single modality. The antibody is the delivery vehicle. The payload can be a cytotoxin, a targeted inhibitor, a degrader, or something not yet developed. What changes is the therapeutic logic — and the computational design challenge shifts accordingly.

For AI drug discovery, this has a specific implication. The design of conjugate therapeutics requires predicting not just binding and potency, but linker stability, payload release kinetics, bystander effects, and resistance mechanisms — a multi-parameter optimization problem where the components interact. As the conjugate category fragments into subcategories, the computational tools required to design them will need to reason about systems of molecules, not individual ones. That's a different kind of AI problem than designing a single antibody or small molecule in isolation.
