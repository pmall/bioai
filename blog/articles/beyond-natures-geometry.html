Protein to Pill

Beyond nature's geometry

2 September 2026

Evolution never built an RNA transporter out of non-natural protein geometries — because it could not. A team at Helmholtz Munich and the Technical University of Munich has done exactly that, producing a class of synthetic delivery vehicles that outperform both virus-like particles and lipid nanoparticles. The result, published in Nature, is significant not just for what it delivers, but for what it says about where AI-designed proteins are heading.

The design

The team calls them Synthetic Transfer Vehicles, or STVs. They are built from AI-designed proteins assembled into structures with geometries that do not exist in nature. The design process used generative protein models to specify protein subunits that self-assemble into capsid-like containers capable of carrying RNA cargo. The key advance is not the assembly itself — biology does that routinely — but the geometric freedom. The protein subunits were designed de novo, unconstrained by the structural motifs evolution happened to produce.

The lead candidate, STV-C8, was tested against the two dominant delivery modalities: lipid nanoparticles, the platform behind the mRNA COVID vaccines, and virus-like particles, which mimic viral structure without carrying viral genetic material. In cell culture, STV-C8 outperformed both.

In animals

The team moved to in vivo testing across two species. Intravenous delivery in mice produced lung expression of the delivered RNA without observable toxicity. In pig muscle, STVs delivered CRISPR/Cas9 cargo that successfully removed the dystrophin gene — the same gene targeted in Duchenne muscular dystrophy models.

The pig muscle result is the more important of the two. Lung expression in mice demonstrates delivery works. CRISPR/Cas9 delivery in a large-animal muscle model demonstrates it works for a clinically relevant payload in a clinically relevant tissue. The gap between "we delivered RNA" and "we delivered a functional gene editor to the right tissue" is the gap between a proof of concept and a therapeutic platform.

What it means for delivery

Drug delivery has been the quiet constraint on RNA therapeutics. The molecules themselves — mRNA, siRNA, CRISPR components — are increasingly well understood. The bottleneck has been getting them into the right cells without degradation, immune activation, or off-target distribution. Lipid nanoparticles solved this for liver targets. Virus-like particles solved it for a handful of gene therapy applications. Neither solved it generally.

STVs are different because they are not constrained to geometries that evolution explored. Natural viral capsids are icosahedral or helical because those are the shapes that worked for viruses. Lipid nanoparticles form spheres because that is what lipids do. AI-designed protein subunits can be specified to assemble into shapes chosen for function rather than historical accident. If the geometry affects tissue tropism, cargo loading, or immune evasion — and the data suggest it does — then expanding the geometric repertoire expands the therapeutic one.

The broader pattern

This is the third time in the past two months that AI-designed proteins have crossed from therapeutic applications into infrastructure. Claude designed protein binders that would have won five of six Adaptyv Bio competitions. AbCellera demonstrated GPCR-targeting antibodies with best-in-class clinical differentiation. Now STVs show that AI-designed proteins can build the delivery vehicles themselves.

The pattern is a shift from "AI designs better drugs" to "AI designs the systems that make drugs work." Delivery is infrastructure. Binders are infrastructure. Manufacturing optimization is infrastructure. The value of AI-designed proteins compounds not when each design is better than the last, but when the designs start composing into functional systems.

Helmholtz Munich is planning a spin-off to commercialize the technology. The distance between a Nature paper and a clinical delivery platform is long — but the design space it opens is genuinely new. Evolution did not exhaust the functional geometries of protein assemblies. AI is now exploring the ones it missed.
