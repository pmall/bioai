Protein to Pill

The bottleneck shifts from molecules to patients

16 August 2026

For the past two years, the dominant question in AI drug discovery has been whether algorithms can design better molecules. That question is increasingly settled. The harder question — and the one the field is now confronting — is whether those molecules can be tested, matched, and approved faster. A cluster of moves this month suggests the bottleneck is shifting from discovery to clinical workflow, and that the companies positioning there may define the next phase of the field.

Pathos AI: agents in the trial

Pathos AI licensed two cancer drugs — JSKN016, a TROP2/HER3 bispecific ADC from AlphaMed Biopharma ($125M upfront), and AZD4241, an AstraZeneca protein degrader — using an agent-based approach to trial design. Pathos's Foundry platform deploys thousands of AI agents to handle what has traditionally been months of manual work: patient stratification, dosing optimization, endpoint selection, and protocol design.

The drugs themselves are conventional. What's novel is the process. Instead of a team of clinical scientists spending months designing a trial protocol, Pathos uses agents to generate and evaluate thousands of possible designs, selecting for statistical power, patient safety, and regulatory feasibility simultaneously. The promise isn't just speed — it's better trials. More precisely matched patients, more informative endpoints, fewer protocol amendments.

This is a different kind of AI contribution than molecule design. It doesn't change what the drug is. It changes how quickly and reliably you can learn whether it works. And in a field where the median time from IND to approval is still over eight years, that matters.

Schrödinger Bunsen: computation for the rest of us

Schrödinger's Bunsen platform, deployed at Bristol-Myers Squibb through their multi-year partnership, takes a different angle on the same problem. Bunsen makes physics-based computational chemistry accessible to non-specialist scientists through natural language interfaces. A medicinal chemist who doesn't know how to set up a free energy perturbation calculation can now ask for one in plain English and get a result.

The significance isn't the natural language interface itself — that's becoming table stakes. It's what it represents: the democratization of the computational tools that were previously locked inside computational chemistry groups. When a medicinal chemist can run their own FEP calculations without waiting in a queue, the iteration cycle between design and evaluation compresses from days to hours. Decision-making improves not because the models are better, but because more people can use them.

BMS deploying Bunsen at scale signals something about where big pharma sees the value. Not in replacing computational chemists, but in amplifying them — putting physics-based computation in the hands of the people who actually design molecules.

Phylo-Chugai: agents in the lab

Phylo and Chugai partnered to deploy Biomni Lab, an agentic AI platform, across Chugai's drug discovery programs. The platform handles single-cell analysis, target evaluation, and experimental design — the upstream work that feeds into clinical programs.

Where Pathos focuses on trial design and Schrödinger on molecular evaluation, Phylo-Chugai targets the biological understanding layer. Single-cell RNA sequencing produces massive, complex datasets. Interpreting them — identifying which cell populations drive disease, which targets are most druggable, which biomarkers predict response — has been a bottleneck that scales linearly with the number of experiments. Agentic AI changes that math.

What these moves share

These three developments — Pathos, Schrödinger, Phylo-Chugai — aren't competitors. They're complementary layers of the same stack. Pathos optimizes how you test a drug. Schrödinger optimizes how you design one. Phylo optimizes how you understand the biology behind it. Together, they represent a shift in where AI is applied: not just in generating molecules, but in the full workflow from biological insight to clinical proof.

This shift matters because the field's central failure mode isn't molecular design. It's the gap between designing a molecule and proving it works in patients. AI can generate thousands of candidates. But someone still needs to figure out which patients to treat, how to measure whether it's working, and how to run a trial that produces a clear answer. That's where the real time and money goes, and that's where these companies are positioning.

The validation backdrop

This shift is happening against an increasingly frank assessment of the field's track record. A Nature Reviews Drug Discovery perspective by Bender et al. published this month argues that evidence of clinically relevant AI impact remains "disappointingly limited." The paper calls for benchmarks that measure whether AI improves actual decision-making — not just whether models predict structures accurately.

The critique is fair and important. But it also describes a problem that the workflow-focused companies are specifically designed to solve. If the bottleneck is clinical decision-making, not molecular design, then the right interventions are in trial design, patient matching, and experimental interpretation — exactly where Pathos, Schrödinger, and Phylo are positioning.

Insilico Medicine's DDD Benchmark as a Service, also launched this month, takes a parallel approach: rather than defending AI's track record, it builds infrastructure to measure whether AI actually improves drug discovery outcomes. Over 300 evaluations using decontaminated program data, designed to distinguish "passing an exam" from "discovering a drug."

What this means for the field

The companies that will define the next phase of AI drug discovery may not be the ones with the best generative models. They may be the ones that solve the workflow problem — that take AI from being a tool for generating candidates to being a system for proving they work.

This is a less glamorous bet. Molecule generation produces elegant papers and striking demos. Clinical workflow optimization produces better trials and faster approvals. But the field's credibility now depends on the latter. With zero FDA approvals despite roughly $60 billion invested, and 15-20 AI-derived programs expected in pivotal trials over the next year, the question is no longer whether AI can design molecules. It's whether AI can help produce medicines.

The bottleneck has shifted. The companies that recognize it — and build for it — will shape what AI drug discovery actually becomes.
