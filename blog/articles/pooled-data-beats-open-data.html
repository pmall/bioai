Protein to Pill

Twenty thousand structures nobody would share

15 September 2026

Five pharmaceutical companies pooled 20,167 proprietary protein–ligand structures they would never publish, fine-tuned OpenFold3 on the combined set, and cleared 50% high-accuracy predictions on 1,056 held-out complexes — against roughly 33% for the public-data OpenFold3 and about 40% for Boltz-2. The pooled model also beat models trained on any single company's data alone. Competitors sharing what none would give away turns out to beat everyone training alone.

Why the public database was never enough

The Protein Data Bank holds around 200,000 structures, but only about 10,000 drug-like protein–ligand examples. That is the quiet constraint behind years of structure-prediction progress: the open world maps biology broadly and drug-like binding narrowly, while the dense, pharma-relevant complexes sit behind company walls. The AISB Network result — AbbVie, Astex, and three others contributing under privacy-preserving pooling — is the first hard number on what unlocking that private stock is worth: a jump from a third right to over half right, on held-out complexes, with the same base model.

It also answers a question the field has argued about in the abstract. Boltz-2, the open biomolecular foundation model used across the industry, lands at ~40% on the same test — strong for public weights, but ten points short of the pooled private model. Open weights keep getting better; private data keeps being worth more. That is the same lesson as Aureka open-sourcing an antibody model that beats AlphaFold 3 while keeping its data flywheel private: release the model, keep the measurements.

Pooling beats hoarding

The result that matters most is not pooled versus public — it is pooled versus siloed. No single company's slice matched the combination, which means the hoarding equilibrium was leaving accuracy on the table for everyone. Privacy-preserving pooling across direct competitors is a new institutional form for the field, somewhere between a consortium and a data trust — and it worked on the first serious try.

The open counterpart is already running: the UK's £8M OpenBind project released hundreds of public structures last month. So both strategies are now in motion at once — pool the private stock behind closed doors, and expand the public stock in the open. The interesting race is which one compounds faster.

The caveats are real

This is a blog post, not a peer-reviewed paper, and the model is not public. Nobody outside the network can check the held-out set, rerun the comparison, or build on the weights. For a field that just got its first blinded antibody benchmark and is learning to distrust self-reported numbers, that is a genuine discount — a press-release accuracy gap is not a CASP-style result.

Still, the direction is hard to dismiss, because it rhymes with everything else in the field right now: surveyed biotechs naming data quality the binding constraint on drug-design AI, validation platforms selling throughput as the moat, pharma operating systems built to close the context gap between experiments. Intelligence is widely shared; measurements are not.

What to watch

Whether pooling becomes a template. If five pharmas can share 20,000 structures once, they can share assays, curves, and failures on an ongoing basis — and the next pooled model will not be a one-off fine-tune but a standing joint asset. The question is who governs it, who gets the weights, and whether the open side — OpenBind and its successors — can keep the public alternative within reach. The scarce input was never the model. Now there is a number on it.
