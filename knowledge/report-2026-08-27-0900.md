## Findings

### AIntibody benchmark: First blinded, wet-lab-validated AI antibody design competition published in Nature Biotechnology (Aug 26)

The complete results of AIntibody, the first international AI antibody design competition, have been formally published in Nature Biotechnology (DOI: 10.1038/s41587-026-03238-6). Widely regarded as the field's most rigorous test — every entry validated by independent wet-lab experiments under identical conditions — this blinded benchmark saw Aureka Biotechnologies take first place in Challenge 1 (in-silico affinity maturation) with AuraIDE, its in-house antibody design model.

The winning antibody designed by AuraIDE reached an affinity of 94.7 pM as measured by KinExA, an approximately 2,000-fold improvement over the parental antibody. Two further antibodies submitted by AuraIDE ranked second and fifth, giving the model three of the top five places in Challenge 1. In addition, AuraIDE designed a total of six antibodies with affinities below 10 nM that also met the competition's developability criteria.

The competition targeted the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of the SARS-CoV-2 Spike protein — among the most thoroughly studied proteins in the world, backed by extensive public structural and sequence data. The competition comprised three tasks: Challenge 1 (in-silico affinity maturation), Challenge 2 (ranking existing candidates by affinity), and Challenge 3 (designing novel CDR combinations).

The AIntibody paradigm applies the CASP-like blinded-benchmark approach to antibodies, establishing the first objective standard for measuring what any given AI model can actually do in antibody design. This is the field's equivalent of what CASP did for protein structure prediction — pulling computational methods out of their own datasets and holding them to a common standard.

### Northeastern capstone study: AI drug discovery frameworks still unable to replace human researchers (Aug 26)

Northeastern University graduate students in Anton Sinitskiy's Applied AI capstone class published findings from three cohorts testing open-source AI frameworks (GPT Researcher, Agent Laboratory, and five more advanced frameworks) on drug discovery tasks. Key findings:

- In the first report (Jan 2026), all eight open-source models failed to draft scientific reports and were incapable of reproducing a human-made drug discovery algorithm developed at Northeastern.
- In the second report (Jun 27), five more advanced frameworks were asked to replicate a Novartis drug discovery report. They were slightly more capable of drafting original hypotheses and full reports, but lacked depth and were rife with errors.
- In the third report (Jun 29), models largely failed at independently reviewing scientific papers, large datasets, and formulas for analysis, producing inaccurate and misleading results.

The students found that AI can be useful for repetitive tasks, statistical support, and stress-testing ideas, but in complex cases it requires constant human oversight. "If you just listen to what is written in the media, on scientific papers, and marketing materials, it sounds like AI is capable of solving all problems, including drug design," Sinitskiy said. "But unfortunately the truth is very far from these overhyped statements."

### Elix + Tohoku University joint research on AI for targeted protein degradation (Aug 18)

Elix, Inc. (AI drug discovery company, Japan) and Tohoku University entered a joint research agreement to advance AI drug discovery for targeted protein degradation (TPD). Elix will apply its structure generation AI to TPD, combining with Professor Ishikawa's expertise in the field. This follows Elix's April 2025 partnership with PRISM BioLab for AI-driven drug discovery for challenging protein-protein interaction targets.
